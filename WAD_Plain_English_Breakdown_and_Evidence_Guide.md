# EU Web Accessibility Directive

## A plain-English breakdown and evidence guide

**Prepared for Double FW Ltd.**  
**Review date: 1 November 2026.**  
**Primary law: Directive (EU) 2016/2102.**

This Directive concerns the accessibility of public sector websites and mobile applications.

It is commonly called the **Web Accessibility Directive**, or **WAD**.

This guide explains who it supports, who has duties, what is covered, and what is excluded.

It also explains accessible outcomes, technical assessment, evidence, accessibility statements, feedback and monitoring.

### How to use this guide

- **Legal requirement** describes a duty in the Directive or its implementing decisions.
- **Practical check** recommends a way to assess an outcome.
- **Evidence** means records that support a finding.

Practical testing recommendations are not additional legal rules.

National implementing laws determine local duties, enforcement arrangements and any stronger requirements.

This guide explains the EU framework. It does not describe every country's implementation or the UK's current public sector accessibility regulations.

### Reading format

Short paragraphs and descriptive headings separate ideas. Lists explain requirements and steps.

Use the linked contents to find a subject without reading the whole document.

Markdown does not fix the display font or spacing. Choose a reader that supports your preferred text size, colours and line width.

### The main difference from the EAA guide

The European Accessibility Act covers specified products and services.

The Web Accessibility Directive concerns websites and mobile apps of qualifying public sector bodies.

**Its core accessibility duty is in Article 4. It does not contain the EAA's seven-part Annex I.**

Supporting rules appear in separate decisions covering statements, monitoring and harmonised standards.

## 1. Purpose and intended outcomes

**Legal references: Articles 1, 2 and 4; recitals 11–12. [S1]**

The Directive creates common accessibility requirements for public sector websites and mobile apps across EU countries.

It supports access to public information and services, particularly for disabled people.

The intended practical outcome is that people can obtain information and use digital public services through accessible interfaces.

Examples include being able to:

- Understand eligibility information.
- Apply for a service or payment.
- Book an appointment.
- Complete and submit a form.
- Read a decision or notice.
- Obtain a usable document.
- Report an accessibility problem.
- Request inaccessible or excluded information.
- Challenge an inadequate response through the enforcement procedure.

Article 4 requires websites and apps to be **perceivable, operable, understandable and robust**.

These four principles are explained in Section 8.

**A technical score alone does not demonstrate that a complete public service works accessibly.**

Assessment should connect the legal requirements, the implementation tested and the person's ability to use the service.

Article 2 allows countries to maintain or introduce requirements beyond the Directive's minimum.

## 2. Which people it supports

**Legal references: Article 1; recital 12. Monitoring context: Decision 2018/1524, Annex I. [S1, S3]**

The Directive supports users generally, with particular attention to disabled people.

Its explanation of disability follows the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities.

This includes long-term physical, mental, intellectual or sensory impairments interacting with barriers to equal participation.

Relevant users include people who:

- Are blind or have limited vision.
- Are Deaf or have limited hearing.
- Have difficulty distinguishing colours.
- Have speech impairments or do not use speech.
- Have limited movement, dexterity or strength.
- Have learning disabilities or other cognitive access needs.
- Have mental health impairments that interact with digital barriers.
- Experience several access needs together.

Dyslexia and other neurodivergent differences can affect reading, understanding, memory and interaction. Needs vary between individuals.

Accessible design can also help older people and users experiencing temporary or situational limitations.

Examples include an injured hand, background noise or difficulty reading in bright sunlight.

### Functional needs and research

The monitoring decision identifies functional needs for simplified website monitoring.

These include use without vision, hearing or speech; limited vision, hearing, movement or cognition; colour perception; and avoiding photosensitive seizure triggers.

This list helps organise assessment. It is not a closed list of diagnoses or an eligibility test for accessible services.

Recruit research participants according to relevant tasks, barriers, interaction methods and assistive technologies.

Include combined needs where relevant. Do not assume one participant represents everyone with the same diagnosis.

The Directive does not make disclosure of a diagnosis a condition for using an accessible website or app.

## 3. Which organisations have duties

**Legal references: Article 3(1); Directive 2014/24/EU, Article 2(1)(4). [S1, S5]**

The Directive covers qualifying **public sector bodies**.

These include:

- The State.
- Regional authorities.
- Local authorities.
- Bodies governed by public law.
- Qualifying associations formed by those authorities or bodies.

### Bodies governed by public law

The definition refers to the EU public procurement Directive.

A body must have all the following characteristics:

1. It was established to meet general-interest needs that are not industrial or commercial in character.
2. It has its own legal personality.
3. It meets at least one of the public funding, supervision or board appointment conditions below.

The third condition can be met where:

- Public authorities or other qualifying public bodies provide most of its funding; or
- Those authorities or bodies supervise its management; or
- They appoint more than half its administrative, managerial or supervisory board.

Do not decide scope from the organisation's name, charitable status or receipt of a single public grant.

### Examples needing an organisational assessment

Depending on their legal status, covered organisations can include government departments, councils, public health bodies, universities and other public institutions.

The Directive does not automatically classify every school, university, hospital, charity or publicly funded organisation identically.

Record the definition and national law used to establish scope.

### Suppliers

A private supplier does not become a public sector body merely by winning a contract.

However, outsourcing a public body's website or app does not remove that body's duties.

The public body should make accessibility requirements, evidence and corrective responsibilities explicit in procurement and contracts.

## 4. Which websites, apps and content are covered

**Legal references: Articles 1 and 3(2); recitals 19–20 and 34. [S1]**

### 4.1 Websites

Covered websites must be accessible regardless of the device used to access them.

Coverage can include information websites, public service portals, transactional services and web applications.

An account or sign-in requirement does not automatically exclude a public service website.

### 4.2 Mobile apps

The definition concerns software designed and developed by, or for, public sector bodies for use by the general public.

It covers use on devices such as smartphones and tablets.

The definition excludes mobile operating systems and hardware.

An app's availability in a commercial app store does not remove the public body's responsibility.

### 4.3 Content and functions

Subject to specific exclusions, assess relevant:

- Text, images, diagrams and charts.
- Forms and interactive controls.
- Search, navigation and sign-in.
- Payments and other transaction steps.
- Downloadable documents.
- Audio, video and media players.
- Status messages, errors and confirmation screens.
- Accessibility statements and feedback routes.

Do not assess only the opening page of a service.

### 4.4 Intranets and extranets

These are websites restricted to a closed group, such as staff or registered partner organisations.

They are not all permanently excluded.

Article 1(4)(g) provides a specific exclusion for content of qualifying intranets and extranets published before 23 September 2019.

That exclusion lasts until the website undergoes a substantial revision.

### 4.5 Staff-only mobile apps

The Directive's mobile app definition concerns use by the general public.

An app intended only for a limited workplace group may fall outside that EU minimum definition.

National law may extend coverage. Employment and other accessibility duties can also apply.

Do not apply the mobile app definition as a blanket exclusion for staff intranet websites.

## 5. What is excluded

**Legal reference: Article 1(3)–(5). [S1]**

An exclusion concerns a specified organisation, website, app or type of content.

It differs from a disproportionate-burden assessment.

**Check national law before using an exclusion. Countries can impose broader requirements.**

### 5.1 Public service broadcasters

The Directive excludes websites and apps of public service broadcasters and their subsidiaries.

It also excludes those of other bodies or subsidiaries fulfilling a public service broadcasting remit.

This is not a general exemption for every publicly funded media or cultural organisation.

Separate broadcasting, equality and accessibility duties may apply.

### 5.2 Certain non-governmental organisations

The Directive excludes websites and apps of NGOs that provide neither:

- Services essential to the public; nor
- Services specifically addressing disabled people's needs or intended for disabled people.

Do not assume every charity is excluded.

Providing one of those services removes this particular exclusion. The other scope conditions still need assessment.

### 5.3 Schools, kindergartens and nurseries

Countries **may** exclude their websites and apps.

However, this option does not exclude content relating to **essential online administrative functions**.

Check the national choice and the relevant functions. Do not extend this option automatically to universities.

Recital 33 explains that accessible essential content on another website need not be duplicated on the establishment's website.

### 5.4 Older office documents

Office file formats published before **23 September 2018** are excluded, subject to an important condition.

They remain covered where needed for active administrative processes relating to the public body's tasks.

Office formats include PDFs, office documents and equivalent formats.

The test concerns publication, not only the date the file was created.

**Practical example:** an older application form still needed for a current administrative process is not automatically excluded.

### 5.5 Older pre-recorded media

Pre-recorded time-based media published before **23 September 2020** are excluded.

Time-based media include audio-only, video-only and combined audiovisual material.

The exclusion does not automatically cover the whole website, media player or surrounding information.

### 5.6 Live media

Live time-based media are excluded.

However, retained or republished recordings need a separate assessment as pre-recorded content.

Recital 27 explains that conversion to accessible pre-recorded content should happen without undue delay.

It indicates that the necessary period should normally be no longer than **14 days**, with narrowly justified exceptions.

That explanation is not a blanket 14-day legal exemption for every recording. Check national implementation and prioritise essential public information.

### 5.7 Maps

Online maps and mapping services are excluded subject to a navigation condition.

For maps intended for navigation, essential information must be available in an accessible digital form.

For example, provide usable information about an address, destination and necessary directions.

An inaccessible map image alone does not satisfy that condition.

### 5.8 Third-party content

The exclusion applies only where the public body neither funds nor develops nor controls the content.

Purchasing, commissioning or embedding a component does not automatically make it excluded third-party content.

Record the body's actual relationship with the content and supplier.

### 5.9 Reproductions of heritage collection items

This exclusion concerns reproductions that cannot be made fully accessible for either of two specified reasons.

The first concerns incompatibility with preserving the item or maintaining the reproduction's authenticity.

The second concerns unavailable automated, cost-efficient methods for extracting text and converting it into accessible content.

It is not a general exemption for museums, libraries, archive services or all cultural content.

Recital 31 explains that relevant requirements should still apply to associated metadata.

Keep the reason specific to the reproduction and the part that cannot be made fully accessible.

### 5.10 Older intranet and extranet content

The exclusion covers qualifying closed-group websites published before **23 September 2019**, until a substantial revision.

Record the publication history and whether later work amounts to a substantial revision under relevant national guidance.

The Directive does not provide one universal numerical threshold for “substantial revision”.

### 5.11 Archives

Qualifying archived websites or apps contain only content that is:

- Not needed for active administrative processes; and
- Not updated or edited after **23 September 2019**.

Both conditions matter.

Calling a current service an archive does not create an exclusion.

An old page within an actively maintained service does not automatically make that service a qualifying archive.

Recital 32 distinguishes purely technical maintenance from updating or editing content.

### 5.12 Other limits of the Directive

The Directive does not automatically cover every private website, desktop application, device or physical public service.

It does not establish a general duty for all commercial products and services. Those may have duties under the EAA or other laws.

**Excluded content can still be requested through the Article 7 feedback mechanism.**

Being excluded from this Directive also does not remove duties arising under other legislation.

## 6. Disproportionate burden

**Legal reference: Article 5; recital 39. [S1]**

A public body must assess whether meeting particular accessibility requirements would impose a disproportionate burden.

Relevant circumstances include:

- The body's size, resources and nature.
- Estimated costs and benefits for the body.
- Estimated benefits for disabled people.
- How frequently and for how long people use the website or app.

The public body must perform the initial assessment.

If it relies on this provision, its accessibility statement must explain the unmet requirements and provide accessible alternatives where appropriate.

### What does not justify it

Recital 39 says lack of priority, time or knowledge are not legitimate reasons.

The same recital rejects using the provision to justify failing to procure or develop accessible content-management systems.

Age, complexity or a supplier's reluctance do not establish disproportionate burden without a reasoned assessment.

### Recommended assessment record

Retain:

- The exact requirement and content affected.
- The barrier and affected user needs.
- Options considered, including staged changes.
- Costs, resources and supporting calculations.
- Expected user benefits and service importance.
- The decision, responsible person and date.
- Accessible alternatives.
- Review triggers and a proposed review date.

These fields make the reasoning reviewable. They are a recommended record format, not an EU-mandated template.

**The WAD does not provide the EAA's separate fundamental-alteration exception or general microenterprise service exemption.**

Do not copy those EAA provisions into a WAD scope decision.

The Directive does not set a universal five-year reassessment cycle for this assessment.

Review its continued validity when circumstances change and follow any national requirements.

## 7. Dates and older content

**Legal references: Articles 1(4), 8(4), 12 and 14. [S1]**

| Date | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| 22 December 2016 | The Directive entered into force. |
| 23 September 2018 | Deadline for national implementing measures. Also the older office-file publication threshold. |
| 23 September 2019 | Application date for websites published on or after 23 September 2018. |
| 23 September 2019 | Relevant threshold for older intranets, extranets and archive content. |
| 23 September 2020 | Application date for other covered websites. Also the older pre-recorded-media publication threshold. |
| 23 June 2021 | Application date for covered mobile apps. |
| 23 December 2021 | First deadline for national monitoring reports, followed by reports every three years. |

The initial website and app implementation dates have passed.

They are not continuing grace periods for new releases.

Always distinguish an implementation date from a content exclusion's publication threshold.

For example, older documents and older recordings use different dates and different conditions.

## 8. The four required accessibility outcomes

**Legal reference: Article 4. Technical explanation: relevant EN 301 549 requirements. [S1, S4, S6]**

### 8.1 Perceivable

**Required principle:** people must be able to obtain the information and perceive interface components.

Practical outcomes include:

- Meaningful images have usable text alternatives.
- Information does not depend on colour alone.
- Text remains readable when enlarged.
- Relevant media information has appropriate alternatives.
- Reading order and structure communicate meaning.

**Practical checks:** assess text alternatives, headings, reading order, contrast, enlargement and applicable media alternatives.

**Evidence:** measured presentation results, content inspections, assistive technology output and recorded failures.

### 8.2 Operable

**Required principle:** people must be able to operate controls and navigation.

Practical outcomes include:

- Tasks can be performed with applicable keyboard and alternative input methods.
- Focus moves in a meaningful order and remains visible.
- Users can navigate without becoming trapped.
- Timing, movement and gesture requirements meet applicable conditions.
- Users can identify where they are and what controls do.

**Practical checks:** complete tasks using keyboard, relevant assistive inputs and mobile accessibility controls.

**Evidence:** interaction records, focus findings, timing checks and recovery results.

### 8.3 Understandable

**Required principle:** people must be able to understand the information and operation.

Practical outcomes include:

- Language and instructions support the task.
- Interfaces behave predictably.
- Controls and inputs are labelled appropriately.
- Errors are identified and explained.
- People can correct mistakes and confirm important submissions where required.

**Practical checks:** review instructions, consistency, input assistance, errors and relevant safeguards.

**Evidence:** content findings, error-state tests and observations of user understanding.

The WAD does not impose the EAA consumer-banking B2 language rule across public websites.

Plain language remains useful. Distinguish good content practice from a specific technical or national legal requirement.

### 8.4 Robust

**Required principle:** content must work reliably with relevant technologies, including assistive technology.

Practical outcomes include:

- Structure and relationships are available programmatically.
- Controls expose correct names, roles, values and states.
- Important status changes are communicated accessibly.
- Apps use relevant platform accessibility services correctly.

**Practical checks:** inspect structure, accessibility properties, custom controls and dynamic updates across supported environments.

**Evidence:** accessibility-tree findings, screen reader results and compatibility records.

## 9. Standards and the legal assessment baseline

**Legal reference: Article 6. Standards decisions and guidance: [S4, S6, S7].**

Article 6 provides a **presumption of conformity** for requirements covered by harmonised standards cited in the EU Official Journal.

That means meeting the relevant cited provisions supports a legal presumption that the corresponding Article 4 requirements are met.

It is not a statement that every part of a large technical standard applies to every website or app.

### 9.1 The verified harmonised reference

The sources checked for this guide identify **EN 301 549 V3.2.1 (2021-03)** as the WAD harmonised reference.

Decision 2021/1339 introduced that reference by amending Decision 2018/2048. [S4]

Its **Annex A** identifies requirements relevant to the Directive:

- **Table A.1:** websites.
- **Table A.2:** mobile apps.

Check each requirement's conditions and whether the relevant function exists.

### 9.2 WCAG is important, but does not cover everything

V3.2.1 draws on WCAG 2.1, including Level A and AA requirements as applied through the relevant clauses.

Its WAD mapping also includes requirements beyond WCAG.

Examples can concern media accessibility features, communications, software accessibility services, documentation and support.

Use the actual Annex A mapping. A WCAG-only checklist may leave applicable requirements unassessed.

### 9.3 Newer standards

ETSI has published **EN 301 549 V4.1.1 (2026-09)**. [S7]

Publication of a new version does not automatically replace the WAD's legally cited version.

Record the current Official Journal reference before making a legal conformity claim.

An organisation can adopt additional WCAG 2.2 requirements as a delivery baseline.

Record those additions separately from the applicable harmonised legal baseline and any stronger national requirements.

### 9.4 Alternative evidence

A harmonised standard provides a recognised route to the presumption of conformity.

If an organisation uses another approach, it still needs adequate evidence that the applicable legal requirements are met.

Do not label a preferred technique as the only lawful design without checking the legal and technical basis.

## 10. What to assess and how to measure it

**Technical basis: applicable EN 301 549 Annex A requirements and WCAG criteria. [S6, S8]**

The groups below explain practical assessment coverage. They do not replace a complete clause-level checklist.

Apply each technical criterion with its definitions, conditions and exceptions.

### 10.1 Content structure and alternatives

**Outcome:** people can obtain the meaning, structure and relationships in the content.

Check headings, lists, tables, reading order, labels and meaningful images.

Assess whether text alternatives explain the information or function. An image having an alternative-text attribute is not enough.

**Evidence:** content examples, semantic inspection, screen reader output and identified omissions.

### 10.2 Visual presentation

**Outcome:** content remains usable with relevant visual adjustments.

For web content, examples of WCAG 2.1 measurements include:

- Ordinary text contrast of at least **4.5:1** under criterion 1.4.3.
- Large text contrast of at least **3:1**, using that criterion's size definitions.
- Text resizing up to **200%** without loss, subject to criterion 1.4.4's conditions.
- Reflow at **320 CSS pixels** for vertically scrolling content, subject to criterion 1.4.10's exceptions.

Also assess applicable non-text contrast and text-spacing requirements.

Record the actual settings and interface states. Do not apply a web measurement to native software without checking its applicable requirement.

**Evidence:** colour values, contrast ratios, viewport dimensions, screenshots and lost-content findings. [S8]

### 10.3 Keyboard, focus and navigation

**Outcome:** users can reach and operate relevant controls through supported accessible input methods.

Check keyboard operation, traps, focus order, visible focus, bypass routes, page titles and link purpose.

For example, test sign-in, opening a dialog, correcting an error, submitting and returning to the previous context.

**Evidence:** repeatable steps, control-level findings and complete task results.

### 10.4 Touch, gestures and movement

**Outcome:** applicable functions support the required alternatives to complex gestures, pointer actions and device motion.

Check controls, cancellation, dragging or multi-point interactions, motion activation and orientation where relevant to the selected baseline.

Do not describe every newer WCAG 2.2 criterion as already part of the V3.2.1 WAD mapping.

**Evidence:** device settings, input method, function tested and successful alternatives or failures.

### 10.5 Forms, instructions and errors

**Outcome:** people can enter information, identify errors, correct them and understand the result.

Test required fields, format instructions, validation, error identification and relevant suggestions.

Check applicable safeguards for legal, financial and data submissions.

**Evidence:** valid and invalid submissions, error announcements, correction results and confirmation records.

### 10.6 Timing, moving content and flashing

**Outcome:** applicable timing and movement controls let users complete tasks safely and effectively.

Check time limits, warnings, extensions, pause controls and visual sequences against the relevant criteria.

The WAD does not set one universal completion time for every public service.

Use technical flash analysis where necessary. Do not expose users to suspected hazardous flashing to test its effects.

**Evidence:** timing conditions, available controls, flash analysis and retest results.

### 10.7 Audio, video and media players

**Outcome:** covered media and its playback controls meet applicable accessibility requirements.

Assess the media's scope first. Then check the alternatives required for that media type.

Examples include captions for relevant pre-recorded audio in video and audio description where required.

Check player operation and any applicable controls for activating captions or audio description.

A transcript is useful, but does not automatically replace every required caption or audio-description outcome.

**Evidence:** media inventory, exclusion decisions, alternative-content review, synchronisation checks and player tests.

### 10.8 Documents

**Outcome:** covered downloadable content preserves information, structure and usable interaction.

Use the document requirements mapped in Annex A, including relevant EN 301 549 Clause 10 provisions.

Check headings, reading order, tables, links, language, images and any form controls.

Automated document validation helps identify some issues. It cannot establish that the document's meaning and reading order are correct by itself.

**Evidence:** file version, automated findings, manual inspection and assistive technology results.

### 10.9 Dynamic interfaces and compatibility

**Outcome:** controls and changes communicate their function and state reliably.

Test loading messages, expanding content, dialogs, selected states, search results and submission status.

Inspect accessibility properties and check what users actually receive through relevant assistive technology.

**Evidence:** interface states, accessibility properties, announcements and task observations.

### 10.10 Additional EN 301 549 requirements

**Outcome:** relevant functions beyond a basic web-content checklist are assessed.

Use Tables A.1 and A.2 to identify applicable conditional requirements.

Depending on the functions offered, these can concern communications, video access features, software interfaces, documentation or support.

**Evidence:** a clause-level applicability decision and test result for every relevant additional requirement.

### 10.11 Activation, identification and conversion

**Outcome:** documented accessibility features can be activated accessibly, applicable biometric use has a permitted alternative, and conversions preserve accessibility information.

**Practical checks:** activate features without relying on the barrier they address. Test permitted identification alternatives and converted output.

**Evidence:** activation steps, identification results and before-and-after content comparisons.

### 10.12 Communication functions

**Outcome:** offered voice, real-time text and video communication meet the relevant conditional requirements.

**Practical checks:** assess the actual communication functions, including relevant text alternatives, interoperability, caller information and audiovisual quality.

**Evidence:** supported-device results, communication logs and quality measurements against the selected clauses.

These conditions do not mean every informational website must introduce a video-calling service.

### 10.13 Authoring, preferences and support

**Outcome:** relevant user preferences are respected, offered authoring tools support accessible content, and documentation and support are accessible.

**Practical checks:** test preferences, content creation, relevant transformations, templates, help and support communication.

**Evidence:** settings, created content, documentation checks and support responses.

Check the exact Annex A conditions rather than assuming all additional clauses are optional.

## 11. A repeatable testing and evidence process

**Legal context: Articles 4–8; Decisions 2018/1523 and 2018/1524. [S1–S3]**

The process below is recommended for public bodies and their suppliers.

It supports the legal duties without inventing a universal EU certification scheme.

### 11.1 Establish the scope

Record:

- The public body and basis for its legal classification.
- The relevant country and implementing legislation.
- Websites, domains, service portals and mobile apps.
- App platforms and versions.
- Documents, media and relevant third-party components.
- Claimed exclusions and their evidence.
- Any disproportionate-burden assessment.

### 11.2 Build the requirement register

Map Article 4 to the applicable harmonised standard requirements.

Add the separate duties for the statement, feedback and enforcement information.

Record stronger national requirements and additional organisational commitments distinctly.

### 11.3 Select a meaningful sample

Include representative:

- Home, sign-in, search, contact and help pages.
- Primary public services and complete transaction journeys.
- Different templates, content types and custom components.
- Documents and media.
- Accessibility statement and feedback mechanism.
- Error, empty, loading and confirmation states.

Explain the sample and its limits. Add pages or screens when a finding suggests a wider problem.

### 11.4 Combine assessment methods

Use methods appropriate to the requirements:

- Automated scans.
- Manual technical inspection.
- Keyboard and alternative input tests.
- Screen reader and magnification tests.
- Document and media review.
- Native app accessibility assessment.
- Evaluation with disabled users.
- Review of statements, support and operational procedures.

Automated testing cannot establish complete compliance.

An audit should identify which requirements were assessed manually, automatically or through combined methods.

### 11.5 Measure task outcomes

Recommended user evaluation measures include:

- Correct completion.
- Understanding of information and decisions.
- Unexpected assistance required.
- Errors and ability to recover.
- Time, effort and avoidable repetition.
- Compatibility with the person's interaction method.
- Confidence and satisfaction.

Define expected results before testing.

For example, someone should be able to submit a valid application and understand whether it was received.

An accessible service may still refuse an application for a legitimate eligibility reason.

Distinguish that decision from a barrier preventing the person from applying or understanding the decision.

### 11.6 Keep conclusions separate

| Conclusion | What it answers |
| --- | --- |
| Applicability | Which legal and technical requirements apply? |
| Technical assessment | Which assessed requirements were met or failed? |
| User experience | Could people complete tasks and understand the result? |
| Operational compliance | Are statements, feedback and other processes in place and working? |

Connect these through evidence references. Do not combine them into a single compliance percentage.

A positive satisfaction score does not cancel a failed requirement.

One successful participant does not demonstrate that all relevant users can complete the task.

### 11.7 Use honest assessment statuses

Recommended requirement-level statuses are:

- **Met:** evidence supports the full assessed condition.
- **Not met:** evidence shows a failure.
- **Not assessed:** the condition has not been evaluated adequately.
- **Not applicable:** a documented condition means the requirement does not apply.

Record legal exclusions and disproportionate-burden decisions separately.

An excluded inaccessible document has not passed its technical tests. Its legal treatment is different.

This is a recommended record, not the statutory accessibility statement.

### 11.9 Evidence example: an application form

**Required outcome:** a keyboard and screen reader user can identify and correct an invalid date, then submit the form.

**Assessment:** inspect labels and instructions, trigger validation, check announcements and complete the correction process.

**Evidence:** form version, expected result, error output, focus behaviour and submission confirmation.

**Finding:** if the error is visible but unavailable to the screen reader, the accessible opening screen does not resolve that failure.

Retest after correction and update the statement where necessary.

### 11.10 Evidence limits and retention

Record the assessed versions and environments. Findings can become outdated after changes.

Use functional segmentation to expose different barriers. Avoid averages that hide repeated failure for one group.

Do not describe a small qualitative study as a reliable population-wide success percentage.

The Directive does not set one universal participant number or internal evidence-retention period.

Follow national rules and organisational retention needs. Protect personal and health information in research records.

## 12. Additional checks for mobile apps

**Legal reference: Article 3(2). Technical basis: EN 301 549 Annex A, Table A.2. [S1, S6]**

A responsive website test is not a native mobile app assessment.

Use the requirements applicable to native software, embedded web content and non-web documents as relevant.

Check:

- Screen reader access to all controls and meaningful content.
- Names, roles, values, states and relationships exposed through platform accessibility services.
- Reading and focus order.
- Platform text size and other applicable accessibility preferences.
- Relevant orientation and input alternatives.
- Screen changes, overlays, dialogs and notifications.
- Identification, authentication and permissions.
- Error recovery and complete transactions.
- Embedded documents, web views and media.
- Documentation and support requirements where applicable.

Assess each supported platform appropriately. Do not assume an Android result establishes an iOS result, or the reverse.

Record device, operating system, app version, assistive technology and settings.

For hybrid apps, assess both the native components and embedded web components.

The national monitoring method treats app versions for different operating systems as distinct apps for sampling purposes.

That does not require separate public statements in every case. The statement must clearly identify what it covers.

## 13. Accessibility statements

**Legal references: Article 7(1); Decision 2018/1523 and its Annex. [S1, S2]**

Public bodies must provide and regularly update a detailed, clear and comprehensive statement.

Use the required EU model as adapted by national law.

### 13.1 Where it belongs

For websites, publish the statement in an accessible format on the relevant website.

For mobile apps, make it available on the responsible public body's website or alongside the app's download information.

It may also be available inside the app.

The model recommends making statements easy to find through a prominent homepage link or links throughout the website.

### 13.2 Required content

The model's mandatory section includes:

- The public body and relevant national legislation.
- The websites or apps covered.
- Compliance status.
- Inaccessible content and the reasons.
- Accessible alternatives where appropriate.
- Preparation date and assessment method.
- Feedback and contact information.
- Enforcement procedure and contact details.

For apps, include version information and date as directed by the model.

### 13.3 Compliance status

The model provides three choices:

| Status | Meaning in the model |
| --- | --- |
| Fully compliant | All applicable requirements of the referenced standard or specification are met without exceptions. |
| Partially compliant | Most requirements are met, with exceptions listed. |
| Not compliant | Most requirements are not met, with the failures or exemptions explained. |

Do not invent a numerical pass mark for these categories.

Do not claim full compliance from an automated score or an assessment that leaves relevant requirements untested.

### 13.4 Explain inaccessible content accurately

Keep the model's three reasons distinct:

- Non-compliance with the applicable legislation.
- A justified disproportionate-burden claim.
- Content outside the legislation's scope.

Explain barriers in language users can understand. Include relevant technical references without making users decode them to obtain help.

Where appropriate, explain how the person can obtain an accessible alternative.

### 13.5 Evidence behind the statement

The statement's declarations must be accurate.

Decision 2018/1523 permits an actual self-assessment, a third-party assessment, or other nationally accepted measures providing equal assurance.

The statement must identify the method used.

The EU framework does not universally require external certification for every website or app.

### 13.6 Reviews

The Directive requires regular updates.

The model recommends accuracy reviews at least annually and evaluation following substantial changes.

The EU model's annual recommendation should not be presented as an identical statutory annual audit requirement in every country.

National law may impose more specific duties.

Optional model content includes improvement plans, timelines, formal endorsement and links to evaluation reports.

## 14. Feedback and requests for information

**Legal references: Articles 7(1) and 9. [S1]**

The statement must describe and link to a feedback mechanism.

Any person must be able to use it to:

- Notify the public body of an accessibility failure.
- Request information excluded under Article 1(4).
- Request information affected by an Article 5 disproportionate-burden claim.

The body must give an adequate response within a reasonable period.

The Directive does not set one universal response deadline in days. Check national law and publish a useful service expectation.

### Practical checks

Test the feedback mechanism itself with keyboard and relevant assistive technology.

Send a controlled request and verify:

- It reaches the responsible team.
- The person receives an understandable response.
- An accessible alternative is usable where supplied.
- The issue is recorded and considered for correction.
- Escalation information is available when the response is unsatisfactory.

### Evidence

Retain request dates, actions, responses, alternative formats and escalation outcomes.

Avoid retaining unnecessary diagnostic or health information.

A contact address that nobody monitors does not provide an effective feedback process.

## 15. National monitoring and reporting

**Legal references: Article 8; Decision 2018/1524, including Annexes I and II. [S1, S3]**

EU countries must monitor covered websites and apps periodically.

Their methods must support transparent, comparable and reproducible findings.

### 15.1 Simplified monitoring

This method detects non-compliance with a subset of requirements on websites.

It must include tests relating to all four Article 4 principles.

It aims to address the specified functional user needs as far as reasonably possible through automated tests.

Countries may also use non-automated tests.

**A simplified scan is designed to detect failures. A scan finding no failures does not establish full compliance.**

### 15.2 In-depth monitoring

This method thoroughly assesses applicable requirements for the sampled website or app.

It must verify every step in sampled processes, following at least the default completion sequence.

It examines forms, controls, dialogs, confirmations, errors, feedback and relevant user settings where possible.

User evaluation may form part of this work. The decision does not require one fixed user research method for every assessment.

### 15.3 Sampling websites and apps

The decision specifies national minimum sample sizes related to population.

Sampling must address a suitable range of public services, administrative levels and geographical areas.

Countries must consult relevant stakeholders, particularly organisations representing disabled people, about sample composition.

App sampling considers frequently downloaded apps, operating systems and relevant current or supported versions.

Later monitoring periods combine previously assessed and newly selected websites or apps under the decision's conditions.

These national sampling rules are not a quota telling each public body how many pages it must audit internally.

### 15.4 Sampling pages, screens and documents

For in-depth monitoring, the decision includes, where present:

- Home, sign-in, sitemap, contact, help and legal pages.
- Relevant pages for each service and primary use, including search.
- Accessibility statement and feedback pages.
- Different layouts and content types.
- Relevant downloadable documents for service types and primary uses.
- Other pages selected by the monitoring body.
- Random pages amounting to at least 10% of the specified structured sample.

If a selected page is part of a process, assess all its steps.

For simplified monitoring, assess an appropriate number of pages beyond the homepage, considering site size and complexity.

### 15.5 Results and reports

Monitoring findings must provide useful information for public bodies to correct identified problems.

Countries report to the Commission every three years, starting from the 23 December 2021 deadline.

Reports include monitoring outcomes, measurement data and information about enforcement.

They must be made public in an accessible format.

This national reporting cycle does not allow a public body to ignore problems between reports.

## 16. Suppliers and internal responsibilities

**Legal context: Articles 4 and 7. The role allocation below is recommended practice. [S1]**

| Role | Practical responsibility |
| --- | --- |
| Accountable service owner | Own scope decisions, resources, barriers and service outcomes. |
| Procurement and contract manager | Specify applicable requirements, evidence and supplier correction duties. |
| Product and delivery manager | Include accessibility in planning, acceptance and change management. |
| Design and content teams | Produce usable interaction, content, documents and media. |
| Engineering team | Implement accessible components and platform integration. |
| QA and accessibility assessors | Assess requirements, complete journeys and retest failures. |
| Research team | Evaluate realistic use with relevant disabled participants. |
| Support and feedback team | Respond to problems and requests, and track resolution. |
| Governance or legal adviser | Review scope, burden claims, statements and national obligations. |

One person can hold several roles. The organisation still needs clear responsibility for each activity.

### Supplier evidence

Request evidence for the actual version, configuration and deployment.

Useful records include requirement mappings, test reports, compatibility information, known failures and corrective plans.

A supplier declaration or product accessibility report can support assessment. It does not automatically prove the deployed public service is accessible.

Test integrations, customisation, content and complete transactions that fall outside the supplier's own assessment.

## 17. Keeping accessibility working over time

**Legal references: Article 4; Article 7(1), (4) and (5). [S1]**

The duty concerns the working website or app. It is not limited to its launch date.

Countries must promote and facilitate relevant accessibility training and raise awareness of the requirements and feedback routes.

### Recommended delivery controls

Include accessibility in:

- Requirements and procurement.
- Design and content decisions.
- Reusable components and templates.
- Engineering checks and code review.
- Document and media publishing.
- Release assessment.
- Staff training and support.
- Feedback handling and monitoring.

Reassess affected requirements after relevant changes to content, components, services, operating systems or assistive technology support.

Maintain the accessibility statement so it reflects known conditions.

Prioritise correction by user impact, service importance and the applicable duties.

A barrier in a low-traffic emergency or benefits service can still have serious consequences.

Prioritisation helps manage corrective work. It does not turn unresolved legal failures into successful assessment results.

## 18. Enforcement

**Legal reference: Article 9. [S1]**

Countries must provide an adequate and effective enforcement procedure.

It must address the Article 4 accessibility duty, Article 5 assessments and Article 7(1) statement and feedback duties.

The Directive gives contacting an ombudsman as an example. The responsible body and procedure depend on national implementation.

The process must support effective handling of notifications and requests, including review of disproportionate-burden assessments.

The statement must link to the relevant procedure for an unsatisfactory response.

The Directive does not set a single EU-wide financial penalty or require CE marking of websites and apps.

Check national law for remedies, sanctions, response deadlines and procedural rights.

Keep complaints, evidence, assessment decisions and corrective work available for the appropriate review process.

## 19. Comparison with the EAA

**Legal sources: WAD [S1]; EAA [S9].**

| Subject | Web Accessibility Directive | European Accessibility Act |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main scope | Qualifying public sector websites and apps. | Specified products and consumer services, plus 112 answering duties. |
| Core outcomes | Article 4: perceivable, operable, understandable and robust. | Detailed Annex I requirements. |
| Scope decision | Public body, website or app, content and exclusions. | Product or service category, operator role, market, dates and exceptions. |
| Main implementation dates | 2019, 2020 and 2021, depending on the asset. | Main application date: 28 June 2025, with specified transitions and national options. |
| Public information | Model accessibility statement and feedback and enforcement routes. | Product conformity records and service compliance information, as applicable. |
| Burden assessment | Article 5. | Article 14 and Annex VI. |
| Other exceptions | WAD-specific exclusions. No separate EAA-style fundamental-alteration provision. | Fundamental alteration and qualifying microenterprise service exemption. |
| Monitoring | Specific EU methodology for national website and app monitoring. | Product surveillance and service compliance arrangements. |

The two laws can be relevant to different aspects of the same organisation's work.

Do not assume that meeting one automatically resolves every obligation under the other.

## 21. Suggested Next Steps

1. Establish whether the organisation meets the public sector body definition.
2. Identify the country and its implementing legislation.
3. Inventory websites, apps, documents, media and relevant suppliers.
4. Record exclusions and any reasoned disproportionate-burden assessment.
5. Confirm the harmonised standard version and applicable Annex A requirements.
6. Assess representative content, interface states and complete service journeys.
7. Evaluate relevant tasks with disabled users alongside technical assessment.
8. Correct and retest failures, recording any remaining limitations.
9. Publish an accurate accessibility statement and operate the feedback and escalation routes.
10. Assign ongoing monitoring, staff training and review responsibilities.

## 22. Glossary and sources

### Short glossary

**Accessibility statement:** a public explanation of a website's or app's accessibility, limitations, feedback route and enforcement procedure.

**Assistive technology:** hardware or software helping a disabled person perform an activity.

**Body governed by public law:** an organisation meeting the legal purpose, legal-personality and public-control conditions referenced in Article 3.

**Conformity:** meeting the applicable requirements.

**Disproportionate burden:** a reasoned assessment that particular accessibility requirements impose a burden disproportionate to the relevant circumstances and benefits.

**Extranet:** a website restricted to an authorised group outside or across organisational boundaries.

**Harmonised standard:** a European standard whose relevant Official Journal citation supports the legal presumption described in Article 6.

**Intranet:** a website restricted to a group within an organisation.

**Legal personality:** an organisation's recognised capacity to hold rights and duties in its own name.

**Metadata:** information describing content, such as the title and description of a collection item.

**Native app:** software using the features and interfaces of a particular operating system.

**Presumption of conformity:** a legal basis for treating covered requirements as met when the specified conditions are satisfied.

**Robust:** able to work reliably with relevant technologies, including assistive technology.

**Screen reader:** software presenting interface and content information through speech or a braille display.

### Limits and maintenance

This guide provides a detailed plain-English explanation. The legal texts and applicable national measures remain authoritative.

It does not provide an exhaustive technical test library or certify any website or app.

Verify scope, national provisions and the current harmonised reference before making a compliance claim.
